# Character performance

In the prompt, write the task the character is performing first, then the emotion. The character first sees, hears or confirms the trigger, and only then shows a visible reaction; end with a choice the next shot can pick up. Express state through small observable actions; do not pile up abstract words like "shocked, cinematic, natural acting".

A reaction can be organized along this chain: entry state → trigger → brief understanding → visible reaction → choice → lingering residue. For example, a character is tidying a desk, hears familiar footsteps outside the door, the hand stops on the cup rim, and after confirming, slowly sets the cup down and avoids looking at the doorway. The next shot picks up this choice and its aftermath.

This is a performance-design reference; not every shot must fill in all six steps. Insert close-ups, empty environment shots or pure action write only what they actually need to express. What a character already knew or decided in an earlier shot cannot be reset in a later shot without reason; in a two-person dialogue, write each person's reception, reaction and speaking order separately, so they do not open their mouths at the same time or swap lines.

## Emotional intensity levels (holding it in outranks letting it out)

Give "how strongly to play it" a ruler from 0 to 10. By default move up the levels step by step, and do not start at full — the performance with the most tension is the one held in, not the outburst:

| Level | What shows |
| --- | --- |
| 2/10 | normal on the surface, only a tiny anomaly |
| 4/10 | the stimulus arrives; breathing, eyes or hands change |
| 6/10 | active suppression; the character tries not to let others see |
| 8/10 | the tipping point; the body gives it away but has not fully let go |
| 10/10 | release, or the reverse — silence; it must have consequences |

Ceiling traits of each emotion (what "played at the highest level" looks like):

- **Anger**: the highest level often sits at 7–8 — the voice drops instead and the body goes still instead, which is more dangerous than screaming; roaring at 10 from the start comes across as cheap.
- **Crying**: strongest in "the second before it can no longer be held back" — the tears can be few; rapid breathing and a swallow that catches matter more.
- **Sorrow**: usually no higher than 5; it relies on empty space, a repeated small action, empty shots and a sense of time, not on wailing.
- **Joy**: the eyes light up first (the first reaction is often to freeze and check), and the corners of the mouth arrive later — not an immediate wide grin.
- **Mirth**: it relies on rhythm and group contagion, not on "everyone bursts out laughing".

## Camera and sound follow the emotion

| Emotional state | Camera strategy |
| --- | --- |
| Repression, endurance | medium close-up or a steady close shot, a slight push in, leaving room to breathe |
| Tipping point | close shot or close-up, locked on the eyes, jaw, fingers, Adam's apple or shoulders |
| Outburst | a medium shot first for the body, then cut to a close shot; do not start extremely close |
| Sorrow and empty space | wide shot, a figure seen from behind, an empty chair, objects and long pauses |
| Joy and mirth | a more open medium shot or group shot, allowing eye contact and body movement |

Sound priority: **change in breathing > environmental silence / ambient sound > object sounds > music**. A scene of strong emotion can do without music entirely; music cannot replace the performance — if the performance itself is empty, no amount of scoring will fill it.

## Micro-action lexicon

Write the performance by choosing from concrete verbs; do not pile up abstract emotion words:

- **Eyes**: dart away, lock on, tremble, widen, narrow, lose focus.
- **Breath**: held, broken, shallow and quick, suddenly let out.
- **Hands**: clench, loosen, hide, reach out, pull back, wipe away blood / sweat / dust.
- **Posture**: lean forward, step back, freeze, slump, square the shoulders.
- **Fabric and hair**: lifted by the wind, plastered down by sweat / rain, brushed by a passing movement.
- **Prop reactions**: a cup trembles, a curtain snags, paper crumples, a bell tinkles, a blade vibrates, sheet music turns.

Cut points land best on the micro-action of a "blink moment" — a breath that catches, eyes moving away, an eyelid flickering, words swallowed back, fingers tightening, steps halting — and the cut then has a motivation.

## Time hinge and performance arc

Before designing a performance, fix the time hinge first: what has just happened, what is happening, and what is about to happen. The performance progresses along an arc of at most 5 levels (for example 2→4→6→8); within one scene the dominant emotional value either keeps rising or is pushed back down at the tipping point — it does not jump back and forth.

Inner activity is turned into visible action, expression, a line or narration, but without adding events that change the story.

Verify in the generated result the causal order of actions, contacted objects, eyeline, voice identity and mouth shapes. Physiological actions and emotional intensity must serve the scene's purpose; use `static:true` for intentional stillness, and the review still judges whether it is the planned stillness.
