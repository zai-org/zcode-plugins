# Pacing by Genre

These are pacing skeletons: where the narration runs fast, where it slows, where it stops, and what it leaves out. Percentages are positions in the narration, observed tendencies rather than quotas. Content (scenes, lines, objects, numbers) always comes from this film's evidence; never borrow wording, facts, motives, real-life backgrounds or behind-the-scenes material from a template or from reference scripts.

How to use:

- Decide the film's type and take its skeleton. A good recap usually hits six or seven tenths of a skeleton but gets the speed curve right. The only self-check: reading it through, does the breathing follow the story's tension, or does it tick at an even tempo?
- For hybrids (sci-fi romance), use the skeleton of the genre that owns the main conflict and borrow only the slow-scene handling of the other.
- General rules: open on a concrete predicament as early as possible, and state needed background plainly; explain a time jump until the listener can follow; when original audio carries a key line, let it enter naturally and do not restate it before or after.
- Commentary, repeated themes and closing reflections have no quota. An ending may stop on a result, a line of dialogue or an image.

## High-concept premise (sci-fi, soft sci-fi, fantasy rules)

For films whose premise fits one sentence: a world that is secretly staged, time as currency, a man aging backwards.

- **Opening (first 5%).** One to three sentences showing that something about the world is wrong: the phenomenon, not the explanation. Small odd details (a hand in a photo, the way people run) hook harder than grand disasters.
- **Setup (5-20%).** Either follow the protagonist's suspicion for several short scenes until the listener suspects too, then explain the premise in one block of two or three paragraphs, only as much as the current plot needs; or state the premise in one sentence early and return at once to daily life. Never scatter the rules across the whole recap.
- **Middle (20-65%).** Anomalies accumulate, one or two sentences each, more and more frequent. Scenes where the protagonist tests the world are built from small repeated actions, each its own short paragraph; this is the genre's slow scene. The motive scene (why he must escape, must gamble) gets a full scene's length.
- **Climax (65-85%).** Escape or showdown: one action per sentence, no commentary, a dozen sentences without a breath until the moment of impact.
- **Ending.** Either explain fully what the premise says about reality, or stop inside the emotion. Not a lukewarm summary that does neither.
- **Avoid:** a rulebook opening before the story; stopping for a lecture at every new rule (story, encyclopedia, story); an ending that half-moralizes and half-teases.

## Suspense, thriller, horror (twist-driven)

Courtroom reversals, cat-and-mouse chases, horror, memory tricks.

- **Opening.** Choose a spoiler strategy and keep it: either reveal the truth in the first two sentences and spend the film watching how the character escapes it, or show only the anomaly and hold the truth to the end.
- **Setup.** A comic or light setup may run long if the first death lands abruptly; the lighter the build-up, the louder the hit. Horror does the opposite: the protagonist walks into the trap within a few paragraphs.
- **Middle (15-75%).** Obstacle and solution cycles of two to four sentences, joined by "but", "just then", "the next second". Every few cycles, a failed attempt written twice as long. In courtroom or confrontation scenes the narration steps back and original dialogue carries the rhythm. In horror, one dry remark may release pressure at the creepiest point, then straight back to dread.
- **Climax.** No warning before a twist; state it directly. Multiple reversals follow one per paragraph with no cushion.
- **Ending.** Stop on the last reversal, cut off mid-action, or pose one question. Never restate or explain the twist that just happened.
- **Avoid:** "but things were not that simple" before a twist (every warning kills it); drifting point of view; walking back through the twist chain at the end.

## Drama and realism (crime drama, biography)

Social-realist dramas, semi-autobiographical films, a character's downfall.

- **Opening.** Either a cold open on a few lines of original confrontation followed by one framing sentence, or start from the film's most dramatic middle detail, show a few strong moments, then go back to where it began.
- **Setup.** Compress the protagonist's troubles into one listed paragraph (rent, a sick parent, a child's needs, a divorce) without expanding each. Each important supporting character gets one paragraph and one memorable trait.
- **Middle (20-70%).** Progress through blows and turns, each a full scene; ordinary days between them get one sentence. **At emotional peaks write actions and objects, not feelings:** a glass slammed down, eating while crying, a return ticket found among the belongings. Analytical asides (a performance detail, a hidden symbol) are allowed if the film shows them, at most two paragraphs each, right after the picture they explain.
- **Climax.** Confrontation or trial scenes lean almost entirely on dialogue; large farewell scenes return to slow narration with long sentences.
- **Ending.** Close on one telling detail, then a short thematic passage, or let it stop on an evidenced detail or line. No call to action.
- **Dare:** to linger on a silence (a cigarette, a back turned) for a whole paragraph; to jump a year in one sentence.
- **Avoid:** equal weight on every scene (three sentences each, a plot list); adjectives for emotion ("he was in deep despair"); more than a few sentences of slogan at the end.

## Comedy and feel-good

Odd-couple comedies, predicament comedies, gentle adventure.

- **Opening (first 8%).** Two or three gags or clever moves in a row without explaining who anyone is, then one question ("why would a rich man hire someone like this?"), then back to the beginning.
- **Setup.** Each side's misfortune in one or two concrete paragraphs, without self-pity.
- **Middle.** Alternate fast gags (two or three sentences each, in volleys) with a slow heartfelt scene every few gags; slow scenes may describe the setting. A signpost sentence may mark where the real game starts. Planted objects appear early and pay off late; in between they are glimpsed, never explained.
- **Climax.** Usually collective kindness or a shared plan rather than a fight: short relay sentences, one person per sentence.
- **Ending.** A clear "the film ends here" marker, then an interpretation that may take a sixth of the recap if it explains one idea thoroughly. A light remark after a solemn close suits the genre.
- **Avoid:** explaining a joke ("this is funny because"); describe the action and dialogue only. Shouting the warmth ("what a touching friendship"); more than one theme in the interpretation.

## War, history, action

Epic relay structures, revenge action, historical ensembles.

- **Opening.** Either a conflict scene through its dialogue (an auction with rising bids) or two sentences of atrocity. A flash-forward line early may set the tone.
- **Setup (5-25%).** Build the bond (person and person, person and animal) through two or three concrete details; everything later depends on it.
- **Middle.** Epics use relays: one section per owner or front, fast inside, hard cuts between. Action films do the opposite: one fight may take a quarter of the recap, every move written out, verbs dense enough to leave no room for adjectives; jokes only as seasoning.
- **Climax.** Big battles fast, brutal moments frozen; sacrifice or parting slowest, even one action per paragraph.
- **Ending.** After the reunion or the revenge, close quickly; at most a few paragraphs of interpretation, perhaps one sharp question with a firm answer.
- **Dare:** to write every exchange of a fight; to put one gentle interlude in the middle of the battlefield and say so; to let the hero suffer fully.
- **Avoid:** battle as a news bulletin with no individual person or action; jokes next to tragedy; turning an anti-war film into "justice wins".

## Disaster, true events, sports biography

Rescues, emergencies, true-story adaptations, endurance challenges.

- **Opening (first 8%).** An odd detail with dialogue, explained a few lines later, or a flash-forward to the finish. Anchor the reality early ("this really happened"), with date, flight number or exact quantities when the film gives them.
- **Setup.** Causes and difficulty in numbers (depth, weight, distance, hours); precise numbers are the suspense.
- **Middle (20-75%).** Plan A fails, plan B fails, plan C; or several attempts that each fail in a different, specific way. Write the failure moment long and the switch to the next plan in one sentence. Question-style signposts move it on ("so how could they confirm it?"). Ensemble emotion goes through original dialogue, a short paragraph per group, as breathing space between plans.
- **Climax (75-92%).** A countdown: short paragraphs and sentences, quick cuts between command, cockpit and passengers; the final push may run on the original shouting.
- **Ending.** Facts first (what event, what happened to the people), then one restrained sentence of meaning.
- **Dare:** precise numbers over adjectives; repetition as the shape of the ordeal; one ordinary family line at the tensest moment.
- **Avoid:** vague numbers ("very deep, very long"); every failure the same failure; sentiment overtaking procedure.

## Romance (including animated romance)

Restrained adult romance, fantasy romance, missed chances and reunions.

- **Opening.** A cold open on one monologue that carries a history, or one sentence that drops the premise. The title and author can wait until the end.
- **Setup (5-25%).** One or two paragraphs establish the couple's history as a debt ("there is unfinished business between them"); the details are repaid later.
- **Middle (25-75%).** The one genre that may run slow overall. Events can be slight; the real progression is the relationship's temperature, and every scene must end with a different distance between the two. Keep flirting and sparring dialogue in the original; narration only moves between scenes. A rival speeds things up on entering and slows them down on leaving.
- **Climax.** A confession, duel or airport chase. **Confessions are quoted nearly in full**, several paragraphs without interruption; a paraphrased love line dies.
- **Ending.** Short: an open image, two sentences, or one line from the film. A few paragraphs of interpretation at most.
- **Avoid:** narrator paraphrase of love lines (use the original or leave it out); "their feelings grew quickly" instead of one concrete scene; a long essay on love at the end.
