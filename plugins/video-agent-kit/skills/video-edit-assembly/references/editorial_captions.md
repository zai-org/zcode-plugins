# Editorial Captions That Add Viewer Interest

Consider short editorial captions while selecting moments, especially for casual family, friends, travel and activity recaps. They can draw attention to a gesture, set up a question or turn an action and reaction into a shared joke. Use them where they add to the experience; there is no caption quota.

For reference reproduction, these suggestions apply only to optional added commentary. Preserve required reference caption treatment and the user's explicit text; do not silently shorten, omit or reposition them to fit an alternative edit. Disclose any conflict that cannot be resolved.

## Start with the Pictures

Find the clearest action, interaction or result and consider what the viewer would enjoy noticing in it. Preserve the setup and response that make the observation work while removing interchangeable surrounding material. Captions cannot supply an action that never happens or justify extending an empty shot.

Choose the voice from the brief: playful for a relaxed outing, affectionate for a child's small achievement, restrained for a quiet memory. Birth memories, ceremonies, grief, serious documentation and performances may work best with little or no commentary. Respect requests for no extra text, exact wording, unobstructed pictures and uninterrupted speech.

Distinguish orientation from interest. "At the park" may help navigation but does not itself create a story or joke. Find the particular contrast, effort or reaction in this footage. For a key optional line, preview its passage with and without text, keeping timing, framing and background treatment fixed. Does it direct attention to a perceptible detail or create a response beyond naming the mood? If it could accompany almost any outing, inspect the action more closely, rewrite it or leave the picture clear. This comparison is useful for an uncertain line, not a quota for every caption.

## Plan a Small Caption Arc

Use the existing section plan. For each candidate, record the visible action and source interval, the caption's role and tentative wording. Assign output times once the picture order and durations are set. Useful roles include:

| Role | What it adds | How to anchor it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Invitation or question | A reason to look for the next moment | Ask about an action the footage actually resolves; do not tease a payoff that is absent. |
| Observation | Attention to a small gesture or amusing contrast | Point to something visible without merely repeating the obvious subject name. |
| Reaction or payoff | A brief shared response after the result | Let the action happen first, then acknowledge the result or expression. |
| Orientation | Context across a meaningful scene change | Use supported location or event information, not an invented itinerary. |
| Callback or ending | Completion of a small idea introduced earlier | Return to the earlier action or phrase only if the closing image supports it. |

For example, ducking under an obstacle and later meeting a lower one can support an observation followed by a callback about ducking further. Keep both visible actions so the connection is understandable. A success caption needs a visible success first. Write in the user's language and natural register, using wording specific to the moment.

Humor should come from timing, contrast or the observed situation. Avoid invented speech, unsupported personal identities, motives or relationships, and jokes that humiliate the people shown. Editorial commentary must not look like a verbatim speech subtitle. Do not label a tentative action as failure, or turn an ordinary expression into a fabricated personal story.

## Make the Text Earn Its Screen Time

- Keep each caption to one readable thought. Use gaps without text so the viewer can attend to faces and actions. Avoid a continuous stream of slogans, explanation or exaggerated excitement.
- Time a setup before its payoff without revealing the answer too early. Keep a reaction near the result and remove it once read. Do not lengthen an otherwise empty shot just to display a joke.
- Choose size, line breaks, contrast and placement at the final output size. Reserve the important faces, hands, sports objects and action result. Reposition or omit a caption when the frame has no safe space. Enlarge or simplify the line before shrinking the picture to make room for decorative text.
- Use a consistent text treatment whose emphasis supports the moment. Static text clips are often enough. For the supported text fields and font checks, use [timeline.md](timeline.md); animated or fading lettering requires a prepared overlay, not unsupported text-clip effects.
- Separate editorial commentary from dialogue subtitles and avoid competing reading tasks. When speech or music is the main attraction, favor the performance and verify the sound before adding commentary over it.

## Review the Captioned Cut

After trimming or reordering, revisit captions attached to the changed pictures. Preserve and retime a useful observation with its visual evidence; if that evidence is removed, rewrite or remove the line. A chapter label does not preserve an observation or callback's function. Decide whether the new pictures benefit from commentary.

At delivery size, confirm that the chosen observation still works without the editing notes. Can viewers read both picture and line, see the result before the payoff caption, and attend to unobstructed faces and actions between captions? Verify competition with speech or music by listening when available; disclose unverified sound.

Remove or rewrite text that merely announces what everyone can already see, misstates an event, blocks the subject or competes with the moment. If the middle remains monotonous, return to selection, shot length and structure. Evaluate audience response separately; adding captions alone does not establish a retention or traffic improvement.
